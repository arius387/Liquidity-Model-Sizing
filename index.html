Sizing the liquidity model

A sweep, a change in state of delivery, a limit entry back into the inverted gap, a two-to-one target. The setup is defined. What is not yet defined is how much of the account rides on each one, and the answer depends on a win rate you have not measured yet.

Three win rates, because one of them is a guess

Ten trades produce a point estimate and almost no information. Eight wins from ten puts the true win rate somewhere between roughly 49% and 94% at 95% confidence, and every sizing decision that follows depends on where in that range the truth sits. The honest response is not to pick the middle. It is to size for the bottom and let the data move you up.

The base case is not arbitrary. The largest single-instrument sample in the record is seven GBPUSD trades at a 71% hit rate, and win rates almost always fall as the sample grows, because the early trades are the ones taken in the conditions the setup was designed around. The floor case is the level at which a 1:2 setup still earns, but only just: at 1:2, breakeven arrives at a 33% win rate, so 52% is an edge with about nineteen points of cushion.

Even the floor case tells you to risk 28% of the account per trade. All three are unusable for the same reason: Kelly assumes the win rate is known, and yours is a number derived from ten observations. It is also unusable for a more immediate reason. Any of these sizes breaches a 3% limit on the first losing trade.

A 3% limit is really a losing-streak budget

On an account that dies at −3%, the question is not what maximises growth. It is how many consecutive losses your size can absorb before the account is gone. That reframing turns sizing into arithmetic you can do in your head:

risk per trade = 1 − (1 − 0.03)1/k where k is the longest losing run you need to survive. Four in a row allows 0.76% per trade. Eight in a row allows 0.38%.

Then k is set by the win rate, because the win rate is what determines how long the bad runs get. Over 100 trades:

Round down, because a real losing run is worse than a simulated one. Stops slip, spreads widen into news, and a partial fill at a worse price turns a −1R loss into −1.2R. The rounded figures leave a margin for the difference between the model and the fill.

Your realised losses so far ran −0.48% and −0.26% of the account. That places your current sizing almost exactly on the floor-case number, which is the correct place for it to be while the sample is this small.

Every entry is planned at two-to-one, but the realised ratio across the record is 1.63 — average win $601.84 against average loss $368.56. Closing the gap between planned and realised R is a matter of execution — where the stop sits relative to the sweep, whether the target is cleared before the reaction, whether partials are taken early enough to matter but not so early that they cap the trade below 2R.

The runner question

You mark the full extension on the chart — the 1:4 and 1:6 the move would have paid if the position had stayed on. That mark is worth more than it looks, but only if you treat it as an experiment rather than a regret.

Holding for a longer target is not free. The stop stays where it is, so the risk is unchanged, but the hit rate falls, because price has to travel further without retracing through your invalidation. The question is precisely how far it can fall before the longer target becomes the worse trade.

Taking your current 1:2 at a given win rate produces a known expected value in R. A longer target matches it at the win rate where p·b − (1 − p) is equal. That gives the threshold below which holding is a mistake:

Those thresholds are the whole answer, and they are testable with the marks you are already putting on TradingView. For every trade, record whether the extended target would have been reached before price returned to your stop. Not whether the level printed eventually — whether it printed first. After thirty or forty marked trades you will have a hit rate for the 1:4 target, and you can compare it against the number in the table rather than against the feeling of having left money behind.

One caveat the table does not show. These thresholds are computed in expected R, which is the right criterion only because the position sizes here are small. At 0.4% to 0.8% per trade the difference between maximising expected R and maximising log growth is under half a percentage point. If you ever size up materially, the longer targets get penalised harder, because their variance is higher and compounding charges for variance.

Two things the per-trade number does not cover

Your positions are not independent

Seven of ten trades are GBPUSD, and gold moves against the same dollar that drives the pound. Two GBPUSD shorts open at once are not two 0.5% risks; in the move that goes against you they are one 1% risk. At an average of 2.5 trades per day, a bad session takes out a meaningful fraction of a 3% budget before the day is over.

The fix is a second limit that sits above the per-trade one: cap the total risk open at any moment, and cap the loss you will accept in a day. At 0.5% per trade, a daily stop after two losses costs 1% and leaves two thirds of the annual budget intact. Three losses in a day on correlated positions is 1.5%, half the account's entire tolerance, spent in one session.

Session concentration is a hidden input

Half the sample is London, and the two New York trades are a sample of two. A win rate measured mostly in one session is a win rate for that session. When you reach 100 trades, split the count by session before you resize — it is entirely possible the setup carries a 70% rate into the London open and something much closer to the floor case into the New York afternoon.

Where this leaves you

  1. Risk 0.35% per trade until 100 trades are logged. That is the floor-case number, and the floor case is where a ten-trade sample belongs. It survives eight consecutive losses inside a 3% limit, and it is roughly where your realised losses already sit.
  2. Add a 1% daily stop and a 1% cap on simultaneous open risk. The per-trade number assumes independent trades. Your book is concentrated in one currency and one session, so it needs the second limit.
  3. Log the realised R on every trade, not the planned R. The 1.63 is the number that compounds. Track it as carefully as the win rate, and treat any trade closed below 1R as an execution event worth reviewing.
  4. Mark the extension, and record whether it printed before the stop. That single field turns the 1:4 and 1:6 marks from hindsight into a dataset. The thresholds are 48%, 40% and 31% depending on which win rate turns out to be true.
  5. At 100 trades, recompute the interval before recomputing the size. A hundred trades narrows the win-rate range to roughly ±10 points. Take the bottom of that range, find the streak budget it implies, and resize once.
  6. Split the count by instrument and session before you trust it. An overall win rate that hides a strong London edge and a weak New York one will size both of them wrong.
The 80% is not a lie, and it may well hold. It is simply not yet evidence. Size for the version of the edge you can prove, and let the next ninety trades decide whether you were being too careful.
